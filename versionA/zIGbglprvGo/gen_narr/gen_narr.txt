In the heart of Hakania, a seminary nestled between Pakistan and Afghanistan, 3,500 pupils receive free education, room, board, and medical care. Some students stay briefly, while others, like Zayed Ula, immerse themselves in religious studies, aiming to spread Islam globally. Zayed, learning English, dreams of a world dominated by Islam and its justice system. Leading the seminary for three decades, Sami Ul-Hak, who once educated figures like Mullah Omar and Osama bin Laden, staunchly defends his mission to spread Islam and recalls past CIA support during the Soviet-Afghan conflict. He insists that the Taliban maintained peace through Sharia law, ending blood feuds and ensuring respect for women. Meanwhile, in Islamabad, the government intensifies its scrutiny of religious schools after a tragic extremist attack, aiming to regulate 35,000 madrasas and close those promoting extremism. The fate of schools like Hakania, which idolize bin Laden but don't teach weaponry, hangs in the balance. Despite limited funding, many Pakistani pupils attend madrasas, including a network founded by a director who reveres bin Laden and Mullah Omar. Rejecting evidence of bin Laden's involvement in 9/11, he promotes conspiracy theories, while Sami Ul-Hak continues to educate students in a strict interpretation of Islam, envisioning a future under Sharia law. How does the Hakania seminary reconcile its mission to spread Islam globally and its reverence for figures like Osama bin Laden with the Pakistani government's efforts to regulate and monitor religious schools to prevent extremism?
